This is a plain-language walkthrough, not legal advice. If you hold client
health information at any scale, have a solicitor read your policies. What
follows is the shape of the obligation, so that conversation is shorter.

## You hold two kinds of data, and they are treated differently

A phone number and a booking history are ordinary personal data. A skin
history, an allergy, a medication list, a patch-test result and a before
photo are **special category data** — the health tier — and the bar is
higher for all of it.

Almost every aesthetic and beauty studio holds both, usually in the same
form, often without noticing where the line falls.

## Lawful basis

You need a reason to hold each kind of data, and "the client gave it to us"
is not one of them by itself.

- **Bookings, reminders, invoices** — usually contract. You need the data to provide the service they asked for.
- **Health information and consent records** — usually explicit consent, and for medical-adjacent treatments, the health-care provision basis. Explicit means a specific, separate, freely given yes. Not a pre-ticked box, and not buried in the terms.
- **Marketing** — always separate. Consent to be treated is not consent to be emailed.

The practical test: could you show, for any given client, which basis
applies to which piece of their record? If not, that is the gap.

## Photos are the sharp edge

Before-and-after photos are special category data, and they are the thing
studios are least careful with. The common failure modes:

- Taken on a personal phone that syncs to a personal cloud account
- Shared in a staff WhatsApp group
- Posted to social with consent that was given verbally, once, two years ago
- Kept indefinitely because nobody decided when they should go

Marketing consent for a photo needs to be separate from clinical consent to
take one, specific about where it will appear, and withdrawable. If someone
withdraws it, you need to be able to find and remove the images — which
means knowing where they all are.

## Retention

You cannot keep everything forever, and you cannot delete everything either —
some records support an insurance or liability position for years. What GDPR
asks is that you **decide, write it down, and follow it**.

A workable pattern:

1. Clinical records and consents: retained for the period your insurer requires.
2. Marketing contact data: reviewed against a defined period of inactivity.
3. Photos: their own schedule, separate from the clinical record.
4. Everything else: the shortest period that still serves the purpose.

The schedule matters more than the exact numbers. An inspector asking about
retention is asking whether a decision was made, not whether you picked the
same number they would have.

## Access

Who in the studio can see the health section of a record? For most software,
the honest answer is everyone with a login, including the Saturday junior.
That is worth tightening, and it is usually a settings change rather than a
project.

## Subject requests

A client can ask for a copy of everything you hold about them, and you have a
month. If assembling that means screenshots, a spreadsheet and a search
through a phone's camera roll, you will miss the deadline the first time
somebody asks. Being able to export a complete client record in one action is
the whole difference between a compliance obligation and a compliance
problem.

## The short version

Know which data is health data. Have a basis for each purpose. Keep photo
consent separate and withdrawable. Write down a retention schedule and follow
it. Limit who can see what. Be able to export everything about one person on
demand.

Every studio owner I meet can tell me their missed-call number. Almost none
of them can tell me their missed-message number, and that is the one costing
them money.

The reason is structural. A missed call leaves a trace: the phone system
logs it, and someone notices the little red badge. A message that goes
unanswered for eleven hours leaves nothing behind at all. It sits in a
thread, the client books elsewhere, and the thread just stops. Nobody files a
report about a conversation that quietly ended.

## Where the volume actually is

In the studios we onboard, inbound enquiries split roughly like this:

| Channel | Share of enquiries |
| --- | --- |
| Instagram and Facebook DMs | 41% |
| WhatsApp | 22% |
| Phone | 19% |
| Web form or booking page | 18% |

Two thirds of the demand arrives as a message. The staffing, almost
everywhere, is still built around the phone.

## Why the inbox has no owner

A phone has a physical location and someone standing next to it. An inbox is
on a device in someone's pocket, and often it is the owner's personal
Instagram login. That means:

- Only one or two people can see it at all
- Nobody is on shift for it — answering is something you do between clients
- There is no queue, so "unanswered" and "answered" look identical
- The busiest hours for messages are the hours you are least able to look

That last point is the sharp one. Message volume peaks between 8pm and
11pm, when the studio is closed or closing. The reply, if it comes, arrives
the next morning. By then a decent share of those people have booked
somewhere that answered at 9:14pm.

## The number worth measuring

Forget response rate for a moment and measure **time to first reply**, split
by hour of day. Almost every studio finds the same shape: a tolerable median
during the working day, and a cliff after six.

> The enquiries you lose are not the ones you handled badly. They are the
> ones that were never handled at all.

## What fixing it looks like

You do not need to answer every message personally at eleven at night. You
need three things to be true:

1. Every message is acknowledged within a couple of minutes, whatever the hour.
2. The straightforward ones — price, availability, parking, patch tests, "can I move Thursday" — get a real answer, not a holding line.
3. Anything that needs a human is visibly waiting for one in the morning, in a queue with an owner.

The first two are what we built Aurelius to do. The third is a staffing
decision, and it is a much easier decision to make once the first two are
handled and you can finally see how many were left.

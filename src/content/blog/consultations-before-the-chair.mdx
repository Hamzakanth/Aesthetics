The clipboard at the door is a scheduling decision disguised as a clinical
one. It puts the intake at the only moment when nobody — client, therapist or
desk — has time to act on what it says.

## What the clipboard costs you

The client arrives five minutes before their slot, fills in a health
questionnaire standing up, and hands it over. Three things follow from that:

- **Nobody reads it properly.** The treatment is already starting. It gets scanned for red flags at best.
- **A disclosure at that point is a crisis.** Blood thinners, recent isotretinoin, a new pregnancy — any of these can mean the appointment cannot go ahead, and you find out with the client already in the room.
- **The patch test is too late by definition.** If the treatment needed one 48 hours earlier, the answer is now to rebook, having lost the slot and disappointed the client.

Every one of those is a timing problem, not a form problem.

## Move each piece to where it can do something

**Health and contraindication screening → at booking.** The questions that
can change whether the appointment happens belong before the appointment
exists. Ask them in the booking flow, and if an answer needs a practitioner's
judgement, route it to one while there is still time to have that
conversation.

**Patch test → a scheduled event, not a reminder.** If a treatment requires
one, the booking flow should offer the patch test first and refuse slots too
close to it. A patch test that lives in a reminder message is a patch test
that gets skipped.

**Consent → the day before, digitally.** Long enough that the client can
actually read it, close enough that they remember doing it. Sign on their own
phone, timestamped, with the wording versioned as it stood that day.

**Photos → in the room.** This is the one thing that genuinely belongs in the
appointment. Take them on the studio's system with the client's consent
already recorded, not on someone's personal camera roll.

## What the therapist sees

The point of moving all of this earlier is what happens at the start of the
appointment: the practitioner opens the record and everything is already
there — history, flags, valid patch test, signed consent, last visit's photos.
The first two minutes go to the client rather than to paperwork.

> The best version of a consultation is the one where the therapist has
> already read it.

## The objection

Owners worry that asking health questions during booking adds friction and
loses bookings. In the studios we have watched, it does add a step — and it
loses fewer bookings than the appointments that currently get turned away at
the door, which are also the ones that cost a slot and a relationship.

Ask the questions when the answers can still change something.
